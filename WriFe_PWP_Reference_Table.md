# WriFe PWP Reference Table

**Purpose:** A structured reference of sentence formulas in progressive complexity, for use across WriFe apps when creating content, designing AI prompts, building lesson examples, or generating practice sentences.

**Coverage:** 28 formulas from the simplest two-word sentence to advanced rhetorical structures, mapped to suggested year groups (Years 2 to 9).

**How to use:** Each formula has a notation (the shape), a pattern with slots (for generating variations), an example (showing the result), and a suggested year level. The "year" column is a guide, not a strict gate. Strong Year 3 pupils may write at Tier E; supportive Year 6 pupils may still be consolidating Tier C.

---

## Notation Key

| Term | Meaning | Example word |
|------|---------|--------------|
| **noun** | a person, place, animal, thing, or idea (subject or object). Includes the determiner if present (e.g. "the dog") | dog, sister, school |
| **verb** | the action; may include auxiliary verbs (is, was, have, will) | runs, was running, will catch |
| **object** | what the verb acts on | the ball, breakfast |
| **adjective** | describes a noun | big, red, tired |
| **adverb** | describes a verb (how, when, where, why) | quickly, yesterday, here |
| **prepositional phrase** | a small unit starting with a preposition | in the garden, under the table |
| **conjunction** | joins words or clauses | and, but, because |
| **FANBOYS** | coordinating conjunctions | For, And, Nor, But, Or, Yet, So |
| **subordinator** | starts a subordinate clause | when, because, although, if, while |
| **clause** | a group of words with a subject and verb | the dog ran |
| **independent clause** | a clause that makes sense on its own | The dog ran. |
| **subordinate clause** | a clause that depends on a main clause | because it was hungry |
| **relative clause** | adds information about a noun, starts with who/which/that | which had been sleeping |
| **participle phrase** | a verb-form phrase acting as a modifier | running quickly, panting heavily |
| **appositive** | a noun phrase that renames another noun | my old friend, a brown labrador |

**Slot notation in patterns:** Square brackets like `[Subject]` mark replaceable slots when generating variations. The brackets are not part of the final sentence.

## Tier C: Sentence Types (Year 3)

Each of these uses the same underlying ideas as Tier A and B but changes the sentence purpose.

| # | Formula | Pattern | Example | Year |
|:--:|---------|---------|---------|:--:|
| 9 | **statement (declarative)** | [Subject] [verb] [object]. | The dog chased the ball. | Y2 |
| 10 | **question (interrogative)** | [Auxiliary] [subject] [verb] [object]? | Did the dog chase the ball? | Y3 |
| 11 | **command (imperative)** | [Verb] [object]. | Catch the ball. | Y3 |
| 12 | **exclamation (exclamative)** | What/How + [adjective] + [noun]! | What a fast dog! | Y3 |

## Tier D: Compound Elements (Year 4)

Joining two ideas at word, phrase, or clause level.

| # | Formula | Pattern | Example | Year |
|:--:|---------|---------|---------|:--:|
| 13 | **compound subject:** noun + and + noun + verb + object | [Subject 1] and [Subject 2] [verb] [object]. | The dog and the cat chased the ball. | Y4 |
| 14 | **compound verb:** noun + verb + and + verb | [Subject] [verb 1] and [verb 2]. | The dog ran and barked. | Y4 |
| 15 | **compound object:** noun + verb + object + and + object | [Subject] [verb] [object 1] and [object 2]. | The dog chased the ball and the stick. | Y4 |
| 16 | **compound sentence:** independent clause + , + FANBOYS + independent clause | [Clause 1], [FANBOYS] [clause 2]. | The dog chased the ball, but it rolled away. | Y4 |

## Tier E: Complex Sentences (Year 4 to 5)

A main clause plus a subordinate clause, in different positions.

| # | Formula | Pattern | Example | Year |
|:--:|---------|---------|---------|:--:|
| 17 | **subordinator + clause + , + main clause** *(complex, sub first)* | [Subordinator] [subordinate clause], [main clause]. | When the dog saw the ball, it ran. | Y4-Y5 |
| 18 | **main clause + subordinator + clause** *(complex, sub last)* | [Main clause] [subordinator] [subordinate clause]. | The dog ran when it saw the ball. | Y4-Y5 |
| 19 | **noun + , + relative clause + , + verb + object** *(embedded mid-sentence)* | [Subject], [relative clause], [verb] [object]. | The dog, which was very fast, caught the ball. | Y5 |
| 20 | **noun + verb + object + , + relative clause** *(relative at end)* | [Subject] [verb] [object], [relative clause]. | The dog chased the ball, which had rolled into the bush. | Y5 |

## Tier F: Sophisticated Modifiers (Year 5 to 6)

Participle phrases and appositives add density without adding clauses.

| # | Formula | Pattern | Example | Year |
|:--:|---------|---------|---------|:--:|
| 21 | **participle phrase + , + main clause** *(start position)* | [Participle phrase], [main clause]. | Running quickly, the dog caught the ball. | Y5-Y6 |
| 22 | **main clause + , + participle phrase** *(end position)* | [Main clause], [participle phrase]. | The dog caught the ball, panting heavily. | Y5-Y6 |
| 23 | **noun + , + appositive + , + verb + object** | [Subject], [appositive], [verb] [object]. | Rex, our family dog, chased the ball. | Y6 |

## Tier G: Advanced Punctuation (Year 6 to 7)

Punctuation that joins, separates, or elaborates with precision.

| # | Formula | Pattern | Example | Year |
|:--:|---------|---------|---------|:--:|
| 24 | **main clause + semicolon + main clause** | [Clause 1]; [Clause 2]. | The dog chased the ball; it caught it easily. | Y6 |
| 25 | **main clause + colon + elaboration** | [Main clause]: [elaboration]. | The dog had one goal: catching the ball. | Y6-Y7 |
| 26 | **compound-complex sentence** | [Subordinate clause], [clause 1], [FANBOYS] [clause 2]. | When the dog saw the ball, it ran, but the ball rolled into the road. | Y6-Y7 |

---

## Tier H: Rhetorical Structures (Year 7 to 9)

Sentences shaped for rhythm, emphasis, and effect.

| # | Formula | Pattern | Example | Year |
|:--:|---------|---------|---------|:--:|
| 27 | **periodic sentence** *(delayed main clause)* | [Phrase 1], [phrase 2], [phrase 3], [main clause]. | After running for what seemed like hours, after dodging trees and leaping fences, after exhausting every ounce of energy, the dog finally caught the ball. | Y7-Y8 |
| 28 | **cumulative sentence** *(expanded main clause)* | [Main clause], [phrase 1], [phrase 2], [phrase 3]. | The dog caught the ball, panting hard, tail twitching, eyes bright with triumph. | Y8-Y9 |

## How to Use This Table in Different WriFe Apps

### For the PWP App
The formula at each lesson (per `WriFe_PWP_Formula_Library_L1-L67.md`) maps to one entry above. When generating a worked example or AI feedback, reference the row number to keep examples consistent.

### For the Resource App (lesson presentations)
Use the **Pattern** column to generate placeholder examples that teachers can fill in with their class. Slots like `[Subject]` invite pupil contributions.

### For AI System Prompts
When asking an AI to check a pupil's sentence, give the AI the formula notation explicitly. For example:
> "The lesson is teaching Formula 17 (`subordinator + clause + , + main clause`). Check the pupil's sentence follows this pattern."

This gives the AI a tight rubric rather than a loose instruction.

### For Worksheets and Templates
The example column gives an immediate, age-appropriate exemplar. The pattern column gives a fill-in-the-blanks template (useful for Support tier worksheets).

### For Assessment Writing
W1 to W6 assessment tasks should target a specific formula band. W1 (easiest) draws from Tier A; W6 (hardest) draws from Tier G or H.

## Pedagogical Notes for Content Creators

**Mixing tiers is fine in real writing.** A skilled writer combines simple and complex sentences for rhythm. The table is a teaching scaffold, not a hierarchy of "good" sentences. A well-placed Formula 1 ("She wept.") can be more powerful than a Formula 28.

**A new tier doesn't mean abandoning the previous one.** When pupils reach Tier E, they should still be writing Tier A and B sentences alongside. PWP works precisely because pupils consolidate older formulas while practising new ones.

**The "object" can be a noun, a noun phrase, or even another clause.** As pupils mature, "object" extends to more elaborate constructions. The formula stays the same; the complexity of each slot grows.

**Auxiliaries are part of the verb.** Treat "is running", "had eaten", "will catch" as a single verb element rather than separating auxiliary from main verb. This keeps the formulas readable for primary teachers.

**Punctuation is implicit in the formula.** A formula with subordinator-first complex sentences (Formula 17) always uses the comma. The pattern shows it; the example confirms it.

**Adjectives and adverbs can be stacked.** A Year 5 pupil writing "The tall, dark, mysterious stranger crept silently and carefully" is using Formula 4 with multiple adjectives and Formula 8 with multiple adverbs. The formula tolerates the stack.
